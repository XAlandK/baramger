NASA challenge · Interplanetary Survival Guide: Martian Map

Baramger: a Marswalk planner for Jezero crater

Baramger is a map that helps two astronauts plan a walk on Mars, stay safe on the way, and do real science at each stop. This page explains the challenge, checks every data source we were given, lists the problems we found, and sets out the plan. The app is now built and tested in two walk zones.

P4: zone Z1, the Three Forks sample depot

Between December 2022 and January 2023 (sols 652 to 692), Perseverance put ten sealed sample tubes on the ground at Three Forks, spread over a patch of flat ground about 50 m across. They are a backup in case the rover cannot deliver its other samples. In this walk, two astronauts go out to find the ten tubes, check them and bring them back.

One app, two zones

Zone switcher

Pick Z1 or Z2 at the top of the map. Each zone has its own data package; the app and the safety rules are the same.

Ten depot stops

Four tasks each

Find the tube, photograph it where it lies, check it is sealed and clean, then pick it up. About 10 minutes per stop.

During the walk

Tube counter

The tablet shows "tubes picked up 3 of 10", and the end-of-walk summary shows the total.

  • Where exactly are the tubes? NASA shows this only as a picture.

    NASA publishes the tube spots as a map image (PIA25682), not as data, and the rover team's sample layer is closed to the public. So each depot stop is the spot where Perseverance parked while it dropped a tube (from its official position list, S1). The tube should lie within a few metres of that spot.

    How we handle it: the app says this on screen, links the NASA depot map, and lists it as assumption A14, "to verify". A clue that it is right: the rover's tube camera took exactly 50 photos at each of the ten spots, which looks like the fixed photo set for one tube drop.

Z1 is a short, flat, easy walk on the crater floor. Most of the time goes to the stops, not to walking. Z2 is longer and steeper, with two short radio gaps.

Browser test of Z1

We walked the whole Z1 flow in the browser: plan, safest route, approve, the walk with all ten tubes picked up, the helmet display, the replay, the walk report and "Send to Earth". Everything worked, with no errors. The test also found these problems, now fixed in both zones:

  • Stop numbers were hidden under the round pins. Each pin now shows its number.
  • Depot names piled on top of each other, because the spots are only 7 to 22 m apart. Names of stops that close together now show only when you zoom in; the selected stop always shows its name.
  • The route line was drawn over the pins. Pins, the crew marker and notes now always sit on top. The route still shows faintly where a hill hides it.
  • Clicking a pin did not always select it. The app now checks whether the click is inside the pin's circle on screen.
  • Smaller fixes: assumption A14 showed a stray ": ." at the end, an old hint stayed on the map, and typing #z1 or #z2 in the address bar did not switch zones.

Learned in P4

  • The weather station has no files for sols 660 and 661.

    Effect: Z1 uses the seven sols 653 to 659 as its weather reference.

  • Three depot sols had more than 800 rover photos.

    Effect: we read up to 800 per sol, so photo counts for some cameras on sols 658, 669 and 670 may be a little low. The tube camera shows 50 at every spot, so its counts look complete.

  • Jezero lies about 2.5 km below the Mars reference sphere.

    Effect: any "is this point behind the planet?" test must use a lower sphere, or every pin counts as hidden. The app uses a sphere 9 km lower, below the deepest point on Mars.

The full list of findings, P1 to P4, is in docs/PLAN.md.

The challenge

From TheChallenge.txt

NASA asks for a map, tool, or screen that a Mars astronaut can use during a surface walk. It should stack data from several NASA missions into one clear view of a small area or one route.

This is the key gap. Tools like Mars Trek and the USGS geologic maps show Mars at a global or regional scale. A single walk covers only a few hundred metres to a few kilometres. Our job is to zoom in to that walking scale and turn the data into decisions: where to go, what to avoid, and what you will see.

LevelWhat is asked
MustA map, tool, or interface for use during a surface mission
MustUses NASA data and tools, from more than one mission
MustA layered view of one location or route
Our ruleUse the data in the Resources section of the challenge
ShouldChoose science goals and a place that fits them
ShouldAt least three kinds of data (optical, thermal, spectral, terrain, and so on)
ShouldPut crew safety first, and show a route that avoids large obstacles
ShouldThink about walking in a spacesuit while carrying tools
MayHabitat screen, tablet, or helmet display (HUD)
MayGround photos from rovers, so the crew knows what to expect
MayRobot or rover support, and astronauts working in pairs

The five resources

What each one really contains, checked against the live services

The text file names five resources but has no links, so we found and tested each one. Here is what they actually give us and how we plan to use them.

R1

JPL Mars Trek

WorksMain data

Map layers for Jezero crater: a HiRISE photo mosaic at 25 cm per pixel, a 1 m terrain model (DEM), CTX images and terrain, THEMIS day and night heat images, TES minerals, global geology, and the Perseverance path. It also has Gale crater terrain.

We use it for: the base map, slopes, heat-based rock hints, and geology tiles. Access is through standard map tiles (WMTS), a search API, and file downloads.

R2

USGS–NASA Planetary Geologic Mapping

Partly checkedMain data

Two useful maps: the global geologic map of Mars (SIM 3292, scale 1:20,000,000) and a detailed map of Jezero crater and Nili Planum (SIM 3464, scale 1:75,000, public domain).

We use it for: the rock unit under each stop and the science story. The 349 MB GIS file has not been opened yet.

R3

Curiosity's proposed route map (PIA23179)

WorksDesign reference

An annotated picture and animation of a planned route up Mount Sharp in Gale crater, released in May 2019. It is an image, not map data.

We use it for: the visual style of our route map: labelled regions, a clear path line, and short notes.

R4

NASA Worldview

WorksInterface ideas

Satellite imagery of Earth. It has no Mars data at all.

We use it for: proven interface patterns: the layer picker, the time slider, and side-by-side comparison. The challenge itself suggests learning from maps of other worlds, Earth included.

R5

Mars Perseverance raw images

WorksMain data

A public JSON feed with 1,055,868 images. Each image has its sol, camera, time, links in four sizes, a credit line, and the rover's site and drive numbers.

We use it for: "what you will see" photos at each stop, taken from the ground at human eye level.

Flags and surprises

16 findings · 4 critical · 5 important · 7 notes

These are the things we did not expect, or that could hurt the project if we ignore them.

Critical

These change the plan

  • The resources point to two places 3,750 km apart

    The challenge's example (boxwork rocks, Curiosity, Mount Sharp) and the route map (R3) are in Gale crater. The only ground photos we were given (R5, Perseverance) are in Jezero crater. One walk cannot use both rovers' ground data.

    What we do: choose Jezero. It is the only site where all five resources play a real part.

  • NASA Worldview shows Earth, not Mars

    It cannot supply a single Mars layer.

    What we do: use it only for interface ideas, and say so clearly.

  • No safety data on radiation, weather, or dust storms

    The challenge asks for safety and "current conditions", but none of the five resources has this data.

    Decided (D2): add NASA weather and space-weather data as clearly tagged extras, after each one passes our five checks. No data linked to China.

  • There is no internet on Mars

    Messages take 4 to 24 minutes each way. A real crew tool cannot load map tiles from a NASA server during a walk.

    What we do: build offline first. Everything the walk needs is packed into one "mission package" before the walk starts.

Important

These shape the work

  • Most layers are too blurry for a walk

    A walk needs detail of 1 m or better. Only HiRISE (25 cm images, 1 m terrain) is that sharp. The geology maps, THEMIS, and TES are 75 m to 20 km. See the chart in the next section.

    What we do: use HiRISE for hazards and route finding, and the rest as background context.

  • No walk-scale mineral data for Jezero

    The CRISM mineral tiles in Mars Trek cover other regions. TES mineral maps are kilometres per pixel.

    What we do: show minerals as regional context only, and rely on the geology map for each stop.

  • Rover photos do not come with map coordinates

    The position field is often (0,0,0). To place a photo, we must match its site and drive numbers to the rover's waypoint list. That list sits on the same NASA website but is not one of the five resources.

    Decided (D2): use it as extra source S1. It is NASA data, and we have already checked it.

  • Mars Trek's rover path stopped in July 2024

    It ends near sol 1219. Since sol 1815 the rover has been west of the sharp HiRISE coverage.

    What we do: pick a walk zone the rover visited before about sol 1800.

  • Very large files

    The HiRISE mosaic is 9.8 GB and the 1 m terrain model is 1.8 GB. The server sends whole files only, and the terrain model has no map tiles.

    What we do: download once (with your OK), cut out only our area, and use map tiles for the photo mosaic.

Notes

Good to know

  • The text file lost its links

    The resource names are there but the URLs are missing, and lines 32 to 34 are broken. The text also says more resources may be added, so the live Resources tab should be checked again.

  • The Curiosity route map is from 2019

    Curiosity's real path has changed since then, and the map is a picture. We use it for style only.

  • Coordinates and heights need one standard

    Sources mix height above the Mars "sea level" (the MOLA areoid) with distance from the planet's centre. We will use the Mars 2000 sphere (radius 3,396,190 m), east-positive longitude, and height in metres above the areoid.

  • Spacesuit limits are our best guess

    No resource gives safe slopes or walking speeds for a suit. Our starting values are marked as assumptions until we can confirm them.

  • Every photo needs its own credit

    Each raw image has a credit line, for example NASA/JPL-Caltech/LANL/CNES/IRAP. The app must show it.

  • The computer needs a few tools

    There is no GDAL, and the system Python is version 3.9. We will use uv with Python 3.12 when building starts.

  • Cesium's ready-made 3D Mars is sharp only at Gale

    Cesium Mars shows Gale crater at 25 cm, but Jezero only at 6 m. It also needs the internet and a Cesium account, and it is not one of the five resources.

    What we do: use the free CesiumJS engine with Mars Trek data. Cesium Mars is at most an optional far-away view of the planet (extra source S2).

Finding a safe route

How the route engine works (built in P1)

  • Cost map. The 1 m HiRISE terrain is turned into a grid of 2 m squares. Each square gets a walking time from its slope (slower on steeper ground, up or down) and its roughness. Ground steeper than 25° is no-go; ground of 15–25° counts as 1.5 times slower, so the route avoids it when it can.
  • Quickest safe path. A standard path-finding method (A*) finds the quickest safe line between each pair of stops, in about a second.
  • Safety checks. The app checks the time budget, keeps a reserve for the walk back, checks daylight and sun activity, and marks where the crew loses line of sight to the habitat antenna (taking the curve of Mars into account).
  • Pairs. Two astronauts walk together (buddy rule).

Data model

schema/marswalk.schema.json · JSON Schema 2020-12

The schema describes two kinds of files. The source registry lists every data source and whether it was checked. The mission package holds everything the offline app needs for one walk.

PartWhat it holds
DataSourceOne resource: ID (R1 to R5, or S for extra), role, access links, credit, date checked
LayerOne map layer: source, kind, detail, coverage, how to load it, what its values mean
SiteThe walk area, its bounds, and the habitat or rover base
ScienceObjectiveA science question and why a human is needed to answer it
StopA point on the walk: type, position, tasks, time, rock unit, photos
Route / RouteLegOrdered stops, path lines, distance, climb, slope, time, and the limits used
HazardA danger: type, severity, shape, the data that proves it, and a short HUD message
PovImageA Perseverance ground photo with its credit, rover position, and camera direction
RoverWaypointA rover position, used to place photos on the map
Assumption / ProvenanceGuessed values to confirm, and a record of every download

Rules built into the schema

  • Every registry and mission package must list all five resources, R1 to R5. We tested this: removing Worldview makes the check fail.
  • Extra sources (S1, S2, and so on) must be marked as "not in the resource list".
  • A derived layer must say which layers it came from and how it was made.
  • Every ground photo must come from R5 and carry its credit.
  • Hazard messages are 60 characters or fewer, so they fit on the helmet display.

Some rules need a small checker program later, such as "at least three different kinds of data" and "every ID points to something real". These are listed in schema/README.md.

Five checks for any extra data

Extra data is used next to the five resources, never instead of them. It must pass every check. If one check fails, we don't use it.

CheckWhat it means
C1 No link to ChinaNot from a Chinese mission or agency (such as Tianwen-1, Zhurong or Chang'e). Not stored in China, and not mixed with Chinese data. No tool or MCP that talks to servers in China.
C2 PermissionA license or terms of use that allow our project. We record the credit line.
C3 VerifiedIt comes from the official source, such as NASA, JPL, USGS, the PDS archive or ESA, and we opened and checked it.
C4 QualifiedAn official product with known detail level, date and accuracy.
C5 RealMeasured data only. Nothing simulated, AI-made or invented shown as real.

Care with MCP servers

  • Use only the three MCPs set up for this project: nasa, pds and gdal. A new one needs your OK and the same five checks.
  • Fix each MCP to one exact version. The nasa MCP is a community project, not made by NASA, and is still set to "latest". We will read its code and then fix the version.
  • Allow only official servers, such as NASA and USGS. No servers in China.
  • Treat what an MCP returns as data to double-check, never as instructions.
  • MCPs only help while we build. The finished app gets its data straight from the checked sources.

Build order

With no deadline set, we build the most important parts first, so a working demo exists early.

  1. Must (P1) · builtThe Z2 data package and the 3D Plan screen, with layers, stops with rover photos, the safest route and the safety summary.
  2. Should (P2) · builtThe tablet view, the walk-through preview, and the weather and space-weather panel.
  3. Could (P3) · builtThe helmet display mock-up, the after-walk replay and the walk report.
  4. Only when you ask (P4) · builtZone Z1, the Three Forks sample depot.

Why CesiumJS? It is built for maps of whole planets and has a ready Mars shape. It shows the sharp Mars Trek photos on real 3D ground, draws the route on the surface, and has a clock for the walk-through and the replay. The same engine shows the top-down tablet map, so we need only one map tool. The helmet display is a simple overlay.

Why not Three.js? It is great for general 3D, but we would have to build the globe, the coordinates and the map loading ourselves.

Why not the ready-made Cesium Mars? At Jezero it is only 6 m sharp, it needs the internet and an account, and it is not one of our five resources.
