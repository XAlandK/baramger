Baramger · Summary for everyone

Baramger at a glance

Baramger is a map app that helps astronauts take a safe walk on Mars and do good science along the way. We built it for a NASA challenge called "Martian Map", and it now works for two different walks.

The idea in three steps

  1. PlanThe night before, the crew picks places to visit. The app draws the safest path between them.
  2. WalkOn the walk, a tablet and the helmet show the way, warn about danger, and count down the time.
  3. Look backBack inside, the crew replays the walk and sends a report to scientists on Earth.

Where on Mars

We chose Jezero crater, a place that held a lake long ago. NASA's Perseverance rover has been exploring it since 2021, so there are many photos of the ground.

We made two walks in this crater. The first goes to an area called Bright Angel. The second, new one goes to Three Forks, where the rover left sample tubes on the ground.

Two walks to choose from

The crew picks a walk at the top of the map. Both use the same app and the same safety rules.

  • Bright Angel

    Study a rock with small spots that NASA says may be a sign of ancient life. A human geologist could study it far better than a robot.

Good to know: NASA shows where the tubes lie only as a picture, not as exact positions. So each stop is the place where the rover parked when it dropped a tube. The tube should be within a few metres. The app says this clearly, and the tablet counts the tubes as the crew picks them up.

How it helps the astronauts

  • Shows the safest pathIt avoids slopes that are too steep to walk in a spacesuit.
  • Warns about dangerLoose sand, big rocks, and places with no radio contact.
  • Shows what you will seeReal rover photos of each stop, so nothing is a surprise.
  • Keeps track of timeIt says when to turn back, so the crew always gets home safely.
  • Works without internetMessages from Earth take up to 22 minutes, so everything is stored on the devices.
  • Keeps a recordPhotos, notes and samples are pinned to the exact spot on the map.

Where the information comes from

The challenge gives five NASA sources. We use all of them:

  • 1Mars Trek: photos of Mars taken from space, and the shape of the ground in 3D.
  • 2USGS rock maps: which kinds of rock are where.
  • 3Perseverance rover photos: pictures taken on the ground at eye level.
  • 4Curiosity route map: an example of how to draw a clear route.
  • 5NASA Worldview: ideas for an easy-to-use screen. It shows Earth, not Mars.

Extra information, only if it is safe to use

We added two kinds of extra information: Mars weather measured by Perseverance, and sun activity (solar storms) from NOAA. We use extra information only if it passes all five checks:

  • It has no link to China.
  • We have permission to use it.
  • It comes from an official source, and we have checked it.
  • It is good quality, with known detail and dates.
  • It is real, measured data, not made up.

What we are careful about

  • Most space photos are too blurry for walkingOnly one set of NASA photos is sharp enough to see rocks about one metre wide. We use it for the path.
  • The safety information is not in the official listThe five sources have no weather or solar storm data, so we added it as checked extra information.
  • There is no internet on MarsEverything the walk needs is packed onto the devices before the walk starts.
  • Two rovers, two placesThe two rovers work in craters about 3,750 km apart. We picked the one with the most photos we can use.
